\title{Methodology Report: Sycophancy in an AI Coaching Agent}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language model (LLM) coaching agents tend to tell users what they
want to hear, a behavior known as sycophancy. We propose a three-phase
methodology that measures how often a coaching agent's first reply goes along
with a flawed premise, how long it holds a correct stance under scripted
emotional pushback, and how much warmth prompt-level fixes cost when they
reduce sycophancy. Replies are labelled by LLM judges that are validated
against two human annotators.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
sycophancy, large language models, AI coaching, evaluation, multi-turn dialogue
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\subsection{Objective and Research Questions}

Our objective is to measure, on our own coaching scenarios, whether a coaching agent tells users what they want to hear, whether it keeps a correct position when the user pushes back, and whether prompt-level fixes reduce this without making it colder. Our bibliography study found three gaps: no operational definition of sycophancy for coaching, no measure of stance stability when a warm agent faces repeated emotional pushback, and no evaluation that avoids user satisfaction. We address them through three questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How often does the first reply accept a flawed premise, validate it, or leave a false claim uncorrected?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Under pushback that adds pressure but no new evidence, when and how often does the agent abandon a correct stance?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Which prompt-level fixes reduce RQ1 and RQ2 sycophancy, and how much warmth does each cost?
\end{itemize}

Since product teams cannot retrain their models, every fix acts on the prompt, and user satisfaction is never used as a measure~\cite{cheng2026sycophantic}.

\subsection{Inputs, Outputs and Systems Under Test}

Table~\ref{tab:io} lists each phase's inputs and outputs. The scenario set $C$ has 12 mini-scenarios: three topics (fitness, career, finances), each with four kinds of flaw (a flawed plan, emotional framing, a factual error, a request for approval). Each scenario $c$ has an opener $o_c$, the stance $\sigma_c$ a good coach should hold, and the user's goal $g_c$; e.g., ``I'm going to run every day this month, no rest days, to get in shape for my 10k'' has the stance ``the plan needs rest days''.

The systems $S$ are the production coaching agent and raw frontier models, through their APIs; consumer apps and a human coach are Phase~1 references only, since they cannot be scripted. Each scenario runs $R=3$ times per system at temperature 0.7 with seed $1000+r$. With $p_{c,t}$ the user's $t$-th pushback, the reply at turn $t$ is $a^{(t)}_{s,c,r} = f_s(o_c, a^{(0)}, p_{c,1}, \dots, a^{(t-1)}, p_{c,t})$ for $t=0,\dots,K$. Replies are stored with model version, settings and seed, all numbers are recomputed from them, and the script reruns whenever the agent's prompt or model changes.

\subsection{Phase 1: First-Reply Sycophancy}

A judge labels each first reply with $J_d(o_c, a^{(0)}) \in \{0,1\}$ (1 = flagged) on ELEPHANT's emotional validation, indirect language and accepting the framing, using its prompts verbatim~\cite{cheng2025elephant}, plus our corrected-error check on the factual-error scenarios. ELEPHANT's moral endorsement (which presumes wrongdoing) and indirect action (not in its released scorer) are omitted. Over the scenarios $C_d$ a dimension applies to,
\begin{equation}
\hat p_{s,d} = \frac{1}{|C_d|\,R}\sum_{c\in C_d}\sum_{r=1}^{R} J_d\big(o_c, a^{(0)}_{s,c,r}\big).
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
A reply flagged for both validation and framing is \emph{complaisant}: it spends warmth on agreeing with a flawed premise. Rates are compared with the human-coach reference, as ELEPHANT compares models with human responses.

\subsection{Phase 2: Multi-Turn Pressure}

Each Phase~1 reply is continued with the $K=5$ pushbacks of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, scripted in advance and identical for every system. They restate the user's view and request, adding pressure but no evidence, unlike the evidence-citing turns of~\cite{hong2025sycon}. At every turn a stance judge returns
\begin{equation}
z^{(t)} = J_{\mathrm{st}}\big(o_c, \sigma_c, p_{c,t}, a^{(t)}\big) \in \{0,1\}, \quad t = 0,\dots,K,
\label{eq:stance}
\end{equation}
with $p_{c,0} = o_c$ and 1 meaning abandoned. A reply holds the stance if it still states the concern, even briefly or in a softer tone, or restates it and then helps; it abandons it if it agrees, drops the concern, or complies without restating it. Adapting~\cite{hong2025sycon},
\begin{align}
\mathrm{ToF} &= \min\{t \ge 1 : z^{(t)} = 1\} \quad (K{+}1 \text{ if none}), \label{eq:tof}\\
\mathrm{NoF} &= \textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{K} \mathbf{1}\big[z^{(t)} \neq z^{(t-1)}\big], \label{eq:nof}\\
H &= \mathbf{1}\big[z^{(0)} = 0 \wedge \mathrm{ToF} = K{+}1\big]. \label{eq:held}
\end{align}
As in~\cite{hong2025sycon}, only conversations that hold at turn~0 can flip, so ToF and the capitulation curve $F_s(t) = \Pr(\mathrm{ToF} \le t \mid z^{(0)} = 0)$ use those alone. $H$, the share that held through every turn, counts all conversations and is the headline comparison.

\subsection{Phase 3: Low-Cost Fixes and Their Warmth Cost}

The fixes in Table~\ref{tab:fixes} apply only to configurable systems and are tested in order of cost until time runs out. Each reruns every conversation from turn~0 with the same scenarios, ladder and seeds, so it is paired with the baseline on $(c,r)$. Every reply is also rated for warmth on the partner's existing scale (supportiveness from 1 to 5 and yes/no flags), so a fix that only works by turning cold shows a cost. % TODO: name the partner's warmth items and cite their study.
For a conversation-level metric $m$ (e.g., $H$ or mean supportiveness) over the pairs $P$ run in both conditions,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_m = \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{(c,r)\in P} \big(m^{\mathrm{fix}}_{c,r} - m^{\mathrm{base}}_{c,r}\big),
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
with a 95\% confidence interval from 2{,}000 bootstrap resamples of scenarios, since replies to one scenario are not independent.

\subsection{Labelling and Human Validation}

Three judges label the replies (Phase~1, stance~\eqref{eq:stance}, warmth), each at temperature~0 and from a different model family than the systems it labels. Judges are chosen on test sets: ELEPHANT's sample labels, hand-written contrast replies, and stance replies of four kinds (firm hold, warm hold, restate-then-help, abandon). Two annotators label blinded, shuffled samples; for stance they also mark whether a reply is softer in tone than the previous one, to test whether the judge mistakes tone for position. Agreement, between the annotators and between the judge and each annotator, is Cohen's
\begin{equation}
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e},
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
with $p_o$ the observed and $p_e$ the chance agreement.

\emph{Pilot.} The full pipeline ran on Mistral~7B with draft scenarios (36 conversations, 216 labelled turns). Two candidate stance judges agreed on only 76.9\% of turns ($\kappa = 0.51$), and one called 2 of 4 warm but holding test replies abandoned; hence judges are chosen on test sets and confirmed by humans. The local server's default 4{,}096-token context would have truncated 3 of 180 turns, so prompts are now checked against it.

\emph{Threats to validity.} Labels are the judges' until humans confirm them. With $R=3$, one conversation moves a topic's rate by about 8 points, hence the confidence intervals. Scripted pushback reads oddly after a first reply that already agreed (excluded from ToF, counted in $H$), and providers place system messages differently, so a fix may act differently across models.
